# Q&A insights and known limits

Loaded when the user asks about applicability, difficult topics, cutscenes, player agency, or negative outcomes — and whenever they ask whether these rules are universal.

## Design judgements from the audience Q&A

**Q-1 · Difficult topics need calendar time.** When two early-game characters were deliberately placed close together to show contrasting forms of racism, the team worried about the first impression, added more preceding characters to the area, and went through many edits. Guidance: writing politics or racism cannot be done in passing; budget extra editing rounds and have people with varied backgrounds read it. The speaker offered no easy general answer — it depends on the game.

**Q-2 · Narrative inside gameplay.** Combat was placed inside dialogue for a prosaic reason (no combat designer, no budget) and a principled one (a detective firing a gun is exactly the moment worth narrating). The design also avoids taking control away from the player. The speaker's own correction: this produced a heavy, information-dense game, and future projects would use **more** cutscenes as pressure-and-release (see D-6). Do not treat "no cutscenes" as a rule to copy.

**Q-3 · When to introduce player choice.** Two layers: fix the main story beats first, but leave room for side content that emerges during writing. In the source project, a small thread about parrots came from a writer's passing fascination. The result feels more alive — but clear the slack with producers.

**Q-4 · World first or gameplay first.** The source project began with the world, then asked which game-design tools (inspired by tabletop RPGs) could tell that story. Skills were shaped to serve a psychological story about emotions and mental states, not chosen for genre convention.

**Q-5 · Regional texture is usually unplanned.** A perceived Eastern-European / post-Soviet quality was largely a natural outcome of where the writers grew up; some characters drew on local politicians. "Originality" often just means the influences are invisible. Guidance to reuse: look at your own surroundings and translate them into the work; a small town is not an empty source.

**Q-6 · Accepting a bad outcome.** Distinguish a bad option the player chose deliberately from a bad outcome caused by a failed check — the second is harder because the player had no warning. Give the player a way to react and reflect: an option expressing horror at what just happened, a companion's comment, a later conversation. Turn the failure into story rather than punishment, and reduce the urge to reload. The speaker said the source game could have done more here.

## Known limits — state these honestly

- **Sample size one.** One speaker, one production, one genre. She described the talk as the overview she gives writers joining her team, and it was her first public talk.
- **Camera-dependent.** Conclusions favour a text-heavy top-down RPG with almost no animation budget. For close-camera or heavily animated projects, start from D-1 and re-derive text density.
- **Self-criticism.** Too few rest beats (Q-2, D-6) and too little reflection after negative outcomes (Q-6) were named as weaknesses by the speaker.
- **Source fidelity.** Oral material came from an English audio transcription; proper nouns were corrected against the published slides. Slide text takes precedence where wording differs. No transcript or slide wording is reproduced in this repository.
- **Not measurable.** Nothing here was A/B tested. Present findings as questions to ask and patterns to try, never as guarantees of retention, immersion or quality.
