# Worldbuilding — setting-stage reference

Loaded when the user works on a setting bible, factions, history, or asks whether a world feels believable.

## Core claim

Believability is a by-product of **unused information**. Real environments contain details nobody will ever explain; fictional ones tend to strip them out for feasibility and visual clarity, which makes the world feel too clean. The remedy is to deliberately produce information that the story will not consume, then let it leak into the writing indirectly.

## Two starting positions

| | Universal / shared world | Project-specific world |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Built to hold | several stories, protagonists and projects | one story, protagonist or project |
| Starting point | no fixed lead; many locations and candidate stories | a lead or a plot already exists |
| Later | — | can grow into a shared world through spin-offs |

**W-1** Classify the project before giving advice. A project-specific world still benefits from three shared-world techniques below; do not prescribe a franchise-scale bible to a small team.

**W-2** When a shared world has many beloved characters, choosing a protagonist is a real design decision, not a trivial one. Surface it explicitly.

## Three techniques

**W-3 · Unfunctional detail.** Add proper nouns and micro-details that serve no plot function: brand names on clothing, shop signs, local slang, an unexplained date on a poster. The point is that the audience never needs to ask. A media-theory observation stands behind this: reality includes seemingly functionless detail to signal that it is an unfiltered sample of the real.

- Anti-pattern: every detail in the setting exists to advance the plot or telegraph a theme.
- Practical dose: at least ten unexplained named details per major location, and resist explaining them in dialogue.

**W-4 · Behind the corner.** Map what the protagonist cannot see: places the player never visits, people who never meet the lead, historical events irrelevant to the plot. This builds an internal information bank.

- Payoff: when an artist asks what to write on a character's cap, the answer already exists; dialogue is written with more confidence because the writer knows the world beyond the frame.
- Anti-pattern: only ever documenting the rooms and characters the player will encounter.

**W-5 · Import real stories.** A fictional world does not need humans or realism to carry human stories. Take a news item or a historical episode and ask how the same story would unfold with this world's rules and cast. News feeds and encyclopedic history are cheap, high-variety sources. This deepens the world and trains the writer at the same time.

**W-6 · Genre test.** Stress-test the setting with genres and leads the project will not use: a romantic comedy, a detective story, a spy thriller. The alternate protagonists that appear in these exercises are not waste — downgrade them into historical figures, minor NPCs, or a short newspaper item a player may find. The world then appears to run on its own.

- Anti-pattern: treating these exercises as throwaway brainstorming. Keep the output in the setting file.

## Setting-stage checklist

- [ ] World type stated (W-1), and the plan for possible future expansion is explicit.
- [ ] At least ten plot-independent named details exist (W-3).
- [ ] At least three unseen locations, three unreachable characters, three irrelevant historical events are recorded (W-4).
- [ ] One real-world story has been translated into the setting's own rules this cycle (W-5).
- [ ] At least three genre tests were run and their byproducts filed as NPCs or in-world documents (W-6).
- [ ] Nothing in the file is a verbatim quote from a source talk or a translated slide.

## Worked micro-example

A port town has a ferry company with a defunct logo still painted on a wall. Nobody explains it. A minor NPC mentions a strike that happened forty years before the plot, in a city the player cannot reach. The player may never ask. Both details exist so that later, when someone needs a name for a union, a brand, or a grievance, the writer is not inventing from nothing.

## Limits

These are techniques for producing writer-side certainty. They are not a licence to dump lore into dialogue — see `design-principles.md` (D-1) and `dialogue-patterns.md` (P-2) for delivery constraints.
